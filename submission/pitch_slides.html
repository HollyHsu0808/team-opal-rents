The problem

Same building. Same bedrooms. Up to $500 apart.

Suburb averages can't see this. A postcode × bedroom rule from government bond data misses by $100 a week.

The flat itself can. Its own past rent and the rents of its neighbours already contain the floor, the view, the noise, the renovation.

The hard part is deciding which evidence to trust: stale adverts, relisted flats, changed bedroom counts.

Method

Four steps, all rebuilt from adverts before each cut-off

1

Repeat-rent index. Pairs of adverts of the same flat give the market move — location and quality cancel out. Held flat after July (Sydney unit asking rents stalled mid-2026).

2

Own history. The flat's latest advert with the same bedroom count, moved to today with the index. Adverts older than 3 years are dropped as stale.

3

Same-building comparables, one flat one vote. Other flats in the building with the same bedrooms; each neighbour counts once however often it relisted; the target is excluded; shrunk toward suburb medians when neighbours are few.

4

Floor view and the grid. LiDAR floor-view difference against those neighbours (coefficients estimated within buildings); parking premium for the second column; snapped to the $5 asking grid.

Rules chosen on window A (May), frozen, then evaluated on window B (Jun–Jul); final model trained on everything ≤ 31 Jul4
What we found

Three non-obvious things the data told us

Old ≠ usefulA flat's own advert from more than 3 years ago is worse than its neighbours' current rents: $53 → $46.5 on window A when we swapped them. 75% of repeat flats last advertised over a year ago.
Same address ≠ same flatWhen the bedroom count changes between adverts (whole-building ids, split lettings) the error multiplies by ~2.4. Own history only counts when bedrooms match.
Rents live on a grid84% of asking rents end in $0, 52% are multiples of $50, 26% of $100. Snapping to $5 took window A from $46.5 to $45.

And one flat that relisted ten times no longer outvotes nine neighbours that advertised once.

What did not work: building-level layers (water +25%, age −6% per decade, flight path −2%, new build −4% against suburb medians) made errors worse — the neighbours' rents already contain them. Rejected on window A.

What we kept: only the floor-view difference to the comparable flats. Window A: share within $100 79.1% → 79.2%; window B neutral ($45, 77.1%), R² 0.877 → 0.880.

Validation

Built only on earlier adverts, tested on later ones

  • No random splits; the code refuses any training row dated after the window's cut-off.
  • Candidates (one-vote strength, stale threshold, snapping, public layers) compared on A only; B evaluated with the frozen rule. The floor-view step was added on A after the base rule froze — both B results are reported.
  • Our estimates sit about 1% above later adverts — consistent with Sydney unit asking rents flattening after July.
Cut-off: nothing dated after 31 July 2026 from any source7
Who it helps · what's next

A rent you can check flat by flat

  • Tenants see whether an asking rent is high for this floor of this building.
  • Landlords set a rent that fits the flats around them — not the suburb average.
  • Investors compare likely rent before they buy.

Next: unit orientation (which way the flat faces), strata sale prices within the building, and a live "evidence card" per estimate: own advert, neighbours used, floor-view adjustment.
